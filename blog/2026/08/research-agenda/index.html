One agenda, three questions

Research areas are often listed as nouns: agents, reinforcement learning, graphs, multimodality. A list is useful for indexing a CV, but it does not explain why the projects belong together. I find it more useful to organize my work around questions.

How can we build agents that learn over long horizons, coordinate with others, and remain faithful to the evidence they use?

This broad goal currently unfolds into three connected questions. The first two describe established lines of work. The third is an ongoing frontier that motivates my current interest in agent reinforcement learning and post-training.

1. How should multiple agents communicate and collaborate?

Question 01 Which communication structure lets a group share the right information without paying for every possible connection?

Multi-agent systems are frequently implemented with a convenient default: everyone can talk to everyone. But communication has a topology, and that topology affects efficiency, reliability, and the knowledge available to each participant.

My work on HyperAgent, EvoHyper, and HyperBalance studies communication through hypergraph structure and topology optimization. D3MAS approaches the same agenda from knowledge decomposition and distribution: collaboration depends not only on who communicates, but also on what information is produced and where it is sent.

The deeper research problem is therefore not simply “multi-agent communication.” It is the joint design of structure, information, and coordination under practical constraints.

2. How can models generalize and reason faithfully?

Question 02 Which structural and evidential biases help a model transfer beyond the shortcuts of its training domain?

Before focusing on agent collaboration, I studied graph learning and foundation models under domain shift. That work remains central to the way I think about agents: an agent is only as reliable as the representations and evidence on which its decisions are grounded.

H4G investigates faithful zero-shot graph inference in hyperbolic space. G²rammar and our work on generic token dominance examine bilingual structure and cross-domain transfer for text-attributed graphs. Related multimodal work studies how evidence can be prioritized when large vision-language models combine heterogeneous signals.

Across these projects, structure is not just an input format. It is a way to state what should remain stable when surface distributions change.

3. How can agents improve through long-horizon interaction?

Question 03 · Ongoing How should an agent assign credit, update its internal model, and improve when success depends on a long sequence of decisions?

Long-horizon tasks expose a gap between producing a plausible next action and learning a robust strategy. Outcomes may arrive long after the decisions that caused them. Plans must be revised as new evidence appears, and collaboration makes the learning signal even more distributed.

This is why I am increasingly interested in reinforcement learning and post-training for agents: credit assignment, reward design, planning, adaptation, and stable self-improvement. Our work on FossilWriter, which learns a hypergraph world model with latent narratives, offers one bridge toward this question, but the broader agenda is still open.

Why write these notes?

Papers present a finished contribution. They rarely preserve the conceptual bridges between projects, the alternatives that failed, or the questions that were useful even when they did not become a paper. This blog is a place for those intermediate artifacts.

Future posts will use three recurring formats: Paper Collision for comparing apparently separate lines of work, technical tutorials for unpacking core ideas, and field notes for shorter observations from ongoing research. The goal is not to publish every unfinished idea. It is to make the reasoning behind the research more explicit, testable, and useful to others.
