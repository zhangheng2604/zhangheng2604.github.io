Paper collision

Does reinforcement learning create new reasoning abilities?

Four ICML 2026 papers study what reinforcement learning changes through distribution sharpening, guided exploration, and mode collapse.

Heng ZhangAugust 3, 20269 min read
On this page Where the question begins Distribution sharpening Typicality bias Mode collapse Empirical support How to evaluate change

Where the question begins

A large increase in pass@1 after reinforcement learning is often described as stronger reasoning. A second explanation is also possible. Correct solutions may already sit in the tail of the base model distribution, while training mainly raises their sampling probability.

Both mechanisms can produce similar benchmark gains, but they describe different changes. The first resembles the acquisition of a new ability. The second improves access to an existing ability. Power Sampling, SAGE, Verbalized Sampling, and G²RPO examine different parts of this distinction.

Accuracy alone cannot establish that reinforcement learning created a new ability. It is also necessary to track where probability mass moved and which reasoning modes became reachable under a fixed compute budget.

1. How much can distribution sharpening recover?

Power Sampling begins with the base model probability of a complete response. It amplifies high probability trajectories and suppresses low probability trajectories.

pα(y | x) = p(y | x)α / Z α > 1

This operation is different from simply lowering the temperature. Temperature sharpens local token probabilities. The power distribution reweights complete trajectories, which allows early choices to be judged by their downstream consequences.

The paper introduces an autoregressive approximation with lookahead estimates. It requires no extra training and no external verifier. On Qwen2.5-7B, Power Sampling recovers a substantial part of the gains associated with reinforcement learning.

These results do not show that reinforcement learning learns nothing. A narrower conclusion is supported. In these settings, some observable gains do not require entirely new reasoning trajectories. Reordering the base distribution is already sufficient to recover them.

2. Typicality bias can hide correct tail responses

Verbalized Sampling shifts attention to preference data. Human annotators often prefer responses that are familiar, fluent, and typical. When several answers have similar utility, this preference can further amplify modes that were already common in the reference distribution.

The proposed method does not update model parameters. It asks the model to list several candidates with probabilities and then samples from that explicit distribution. Across creative writing, dialogue simulation, and synthetic data tasks, the method recovers more diverse outputs without a corresponding loss in overall quality.

The absence of a mode from a default response is therefore weak evidence that the model does not represent it. Prompt format, preference data, and decoding all influence which abilities become visible.

3. Correct modes can compete under GRPO

G²RPO treats different correct solution families as modes in a probability space. Its mean field analysis shows that standard GRPO increases concentration within the distribution over correct modes.

Ly = ||y||22

As this quantity approaches 1, probability mass concentrates on one correct solution family. Token entropy may remain high and the surface text may still vary, even though the underlying strategies have narrowed.

G²RPO adds an advantage bonus related to the inverse probability of a rare correct mode. On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, GRPO reduces the average number of correct modes from 2.35 in the base model to 1.97. G²RPO raises it to 6.40. AIME 2025 accuracy also rises from 46.7 percent under GRPO to 48.9 percent.

The modes are estimated with embeddings and DBSCAN, so absolute counts depend on the encoder and clustering radius. The strongest evidence is the relative trend under a fixed proxy. GRPO becomes more concentrated while G²RPO becomes more dispersed without losing accuracy.

4. Empirical support expansion is not ability creation

SAGE studies the tension between stability and exploration under reverse KL. Anchoring the training policy to a reference model can reduce reward hacking, but it also suppresses rare trajectories from that reference model.

π*(y | x) ∝ πref(y | x) exp(r / β) π*SAGE(y | x) ∝ q(x, y) πref(y | x) exp(r / β)

SAGE uses a guide function to reshape the anchor and encourages exploration near uncertain reasoning branches. A trajectory with nonzero but extremely small probability can then cross the visibility threshold imposed by a finite rollout budget.

The method expands empirical support rather than theoretical support. It cannot introduce trajectories that have zero probability under the reference policy. SAGE is therefore better understood as improving access to tail abilities than as direct evidence of entirely new abilities.

5. A fuller evaluation of ability change

The four papers suggest a three level description of improvement.

  1. Probability reordering. A correct mode already exists and training or inference time sharpening improves its rank.
  2. Improved reachability. A mode exists but rarely appears under a fixed sampling budget. Guided exploration moves it into empirical support.
  3. Representational or algorithmic expansion. The model acquires a solution structure that the base model cannot recover under a reasonable compute budget and transfers it to new task distributions.

The third level is closest to what is usually meant by a new reasoning ability, and it is the hardest to establish. Almost every finite token sequence can have nonzero probability under a softmax language model. Meaningful claims therefore need a fixed budget for sampling, context, and verification.

A useful evaluation should report at least four groups of measurements.

  • pass@1 for single sample accuracy
  • the full pass@k curve for coverage
  • solution family count, entropy, and concentration under semantic or structural clustering
  • recoverability from the base model under stronger sampling and larger inference budgets, followed by out of distribution transfer

If resampling lets the base model match an RL model while the RL model loses pass@k and mode entropy, the evidence supports more efficient ability selection. Evidence for ability expansion becomes stronger only when new solution families appear reliably under a fixed budget and cannot be recovered from the base model through inference scaling.
